# Writing a Deadpan script

Read [`reference/OWNER-BRIEF.md`](reference/OWNER-BRIEF.md) first — the owner's own formula, verbatim. This page is
that formula turned into something a writer can fill in, plus the places where our engine changes the plan.

## 1. Where a premise comes from

Take a **mundane idiom, childhood game, or everyday rule** and make it a literal, inescapable law that every
character accepts without question.

| Rule | What it becomes |
|---|---|
| "Finders keepers" | Federal law. A lawyer argues a lost glove in a courtroom with a flag and a jury |
| "30 minutes or it's free" | A 40-year game of tag between every driver and every customer |
| "The customer is always right" | A legal charter. A man is arrested for disagreeing about a sandwich |
| "You break it, you buy it" | Debt that follows a family for generations |
| "Last one there's a rotten egg" | A town that has not slept since 1998 |

The protagonist is a **relatable first-person everyman**: a renter, a delivery driver, a regular guy, a kid with a
job. Never a hero, never chosen. The world is normal to everybody in it. Only the viewer is allowed to find it
strange.

**Minecraft gives you the twist the Roblox films do not have.** The rule should be *made of a Minecraft mechanic*:
mob pathfinding, spawn rules, villager trades, iron golem aggro, redstone, hunger, bed spawns, the day-night clock,
a stack of 64. That is the thing the audience will screenshot.

> Good: "In this village, every trade is legally binding, and I traded my house for a single emerald."
> Bad: a generic office comedy with Minecraft skins on it.

## 2. Sentence rules

- **340–420 words.** 1:40–2:12 at 190–230 words a minute.
- **5–14 words a sentence.** Short, active, past tense.
- **No dialogue tags with feeling.** "He exclaimed", "she replied angrily" — never. Put the line in the sentence and
  let it stand: *"He said my rent was late. I said I knew."*
- **Exact numbers, days, props.** $400. 999 deliveries. Seven penguins. Tuesday's sandwich. A number that could be
  wrong is a joke that did not land.
- **Never wink.** No "and that is when things got weird", no "you won't believe", no "crazy". The narrator believes
  he is telling you a normal story about a normal week.
- **One dry practical one-liner every 2–3 beats.** Not a punchline every line; a shrug every so often. *"I respected
  the paperwork."*

## 3. The six beats, with budgets

| # | Beat | Words | What it does | Fill in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook, mid-action | 20–35 | A mundane act, an absurd reaction, no greeting. Sentence one is also the title | *"I ___ . So ___ ."* |
| 2 | Rule + stakes + the planted detail | 55–80 | The law of the world, the clock, and one small physical thing the climax will use | *"In this ___ , ___ . I had until ___ . I kept ___ in my pocket."* |
| 3 | The ladder | 130–190 | 5–7 stages, each worse, each anchored to a time or a count, each a new place | *"The next day… / 21 minutes left… / By Friday…"* |
| 4 | The pivot | 25–40 | "So that was it. ___ . So how do you ___ ? I simply ___ ." | The realisation, then the move |
| 5 | The loophole | 55–85 | The antagonist's own rule or reflex defeats them | *"Their rule said ___ . So I ___ ."* |
| 6 | The button | 20–35 | Confirm the win, then the ironic twist that loops the premise | *"It worked. ___ ."* |

**Beat 2 is where most scripts die.** The planted detail must be *boring* when you place it: a receipt, a habit of
knocking twice, a spare key under a mat, a name on a list. If the viewer notices it as important, the ending is not a
payoff, it is a wait.

## 4. The ladder (beat 3) is the retention engine

- **5–7 stages, never 4, never 9.** Each stage is one location and one escalation. Cut the same beat twice and the
  viewer leaves.
- **Anchor every stage to a number.** Time left, deliveries done, people in the room, dollars owed. A running count is
  what makes a viewer stay to the end: they want to see what the number reaches.
- **Escalate the size, not the volume.** The reaction stays flat; the situation grows. Shouting is not escalation.
- **One place per stage.** The camera language of this topic (see [`SHOTS.md`](SHOTS.md)) needs a new room every few
  seconds or the micro-cuts have nothing to cut to.

## 5. The pivot (beat 4)

Leave this pattern almost word for word; it is the retention lock:

> "So that was it. **He never reads anything.** So how do you get out of a contract with a man who cannot read? I
> simply **put it in the one place he always looks**."

First sentence = the hidden pattern. Second = the impossible goal stated as a question. Third = the planted detail from
beat 2. Nothing else belongs in this beat.

## 6. The button (beat 6)

The goal succeeds. Then, within two sentences, one ironic consequence:

> "The debt was cleared. My landlord moved out on Sunday. On Monday his brother moved in, with the same paperwork."

Do not moralise. Do not explain the joke. The film ends on the flat line.

## 7. Before you record

- [ ] 340–420 words, and every sentence is 5–14 words.
- [ ] Sentence one is mid-action, has no greeting, and is the title of the film.
- [ ] The rule is stated in one plain sentence by the end of beat 2, and it is a Minecraft rule.
- [ ] One boring physical detail is planted in beat 2 and used in beat 5.
- [ ] 5–7 ladder stages, every one anchored to a number or a day, every one in a new place.
- [ ] The pivot uses the pattern in section 5, at about 70% of the runtime.
- [ ] The last line is ironic, not a moral, and the word "subscribe" is in the final two sentences.
- [ ] Nobody in the film thinks anything is strange. Read it back: if the narrator ever sounds surprised, cut it.
