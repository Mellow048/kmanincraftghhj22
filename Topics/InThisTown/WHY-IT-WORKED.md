# Why "Monster Village" got 5.5k and the shadow film did not

The owner's question, 2026-11-12: *"that video got me like 5.5k views on shorts... i have no idea what i did there...
i need you to study that short and make a similar one... increase our stayed to watch to atleast above 75%."*

For a new Short use the [Nov 18 study of the delivered Part 1](reference/monster-village/NEW-SHORT.md). The figures and hypotheses below are historical; average percentage viewed and stayed-to-watch are different measures.

This is the audit. It is written from `src/videos/in-this-town-monster-village/` and it exists so the next films copy
the machinery instead of the plot.

## The numbers, side by side

| shots | 74 | 62 |
| words | 713 | 489 |

The single biggest mechanical difference is the last-but-three line. Monster Village **changes situation every
eight seconds** — a new place, a new character, a new object, a new idea — twenty-two times. The shadow film sits in
one classroom for the first third of its run. Same shot count, same words a minute, half the novelty.

## What it actually does, act by act

**The first seven seconds carry four lines and two questions.** "I was the only one who was not a monster." (a
contradiction: it should be the other way round). "My mum was a zombie. My dad was a skeleton." (the contradiction
is a *family*, not a world). "And I was a boy they found in a basket." (a mystery he does not explain). The viewer
has two unanswered questions before the eighth second, and neither is a riddle — both are concrete facts that
happen to be impossible.

**Every world line is a rule plus a punchline, in under three and a half seconds.** "Our neighbour Hiss was a
creeper. He loved hugs. Nobody wanted one." "The school bus was a slime. You got in. You got out wet." Two of these
in a row teach the audience the rhythm of the film: *this town will keep handing you a joke every few seconds*. That
is a promise, and it is why people stay past ten seconds. In the shadow film the equivalent lines are "Nobody knew
why. Not even the doctor." — a stall, not a gag.

**The second character arrives as a puzzle, not an introduction.** Mo is a zombie in a straw hat with a scarf up to
his nose who stares too long. Four of his quirks are **plants** — he sits in the sun, he carries a lantern, he eats
bread, he sneezes — and each one is a tiny wrong note the viewer files away without being told to. Then a fifth:
"We always knew. Zombies do not eat bread."

**The hero misreads the town, and the viewer gets ahead of him.** "At sunset the whole village walked me home. Then
they locked my door. From the outside. I thought it was because I was the weak one." Eight seconds later he finds
them standing in a ring, *facing out*. The pleasure is not the twist, it is the two beats where you are smarter than
the narrator. That is what makes people comment, and comments are half of what pushes a short.

**It escalates nine times.** curiosity → gags → friendship → a rule → a rule broken → torches on the hill → the
charge → the sword over his mother → the catch. Each one is *bigger and faster* than the last, and the jokes get
quieter as it goes, so the turn to violence lands.

**The reveal is paid for by a list.** "The bread. The lantern. The sneeze. The sun." Four planted details restated in
one line, then the line that re-reads them: "Who do you think carried the basket here?" This is the cheapest, most
reliable emotional hit in the whole form — the audience is rewarded for having paid attention, on camera, in five
seconds.

**The threat reverses.** The players did not come for the village, they came for Mo. Then a monster wins the fight
with one word — "Hug." — which is a callback to a joke in the second act. Comedy that becomes the weapon.

**And then it does the thing that made the views.** With thirty seconds left it *adds a bigger threat* — hundreds of
players out of the trees — and takes the friend away: "Mo was not with us... He put on an iron helmet. An iron
chestplate. And he drew a sword." Then the ask: "Like and subscribe for part two." The ask works because the story
is visibly unfinished. It is a promise, not a beg.

## What the shadow film got wrong, specifically

1. **Nine situations for a hundred and sixteen seconds.** Half of them are the same classroom. Fix: one new place or
   object every 7–9 seconds, twenty-plus beats, never two shots in the same set-up in a row.
2. **No stakes and no threat.** The shadow is a gimmick: it can't hurt him. Monster Village has swords. A film with
   nothing at risk has nothing to stay for.
3. **No secret to find.** The shadow's rule is explained in line two ("it had a mind of its own") and never
   re-read. Fix: plant three to five quirks and pay them off in one list line near the end.
4. **The narrator never misreads anything.** He is scared, then brave, then happy — a straight line. Fix: let him
   believe something wrong for a full act, then show the truth.
5. **The ending explains instead of revealing.** "It was the part of me that was not scared" is a thesis. Monster
   Village ends on an image: a friend alone in the street, putting on armour.
6. **The last line asks for a like with no unfinished story behind it.** Fine once, in a one-shot, as the owner asked
   — but it will not carry a series.

## The formula to copy (not the plot)

```
HOOK      0:00–0:07   4 shots. A contradiction, a family, the self, a mystery object.
WORLD     0:07–0:35   6–8 shots. Rule + punchline. 2.5 s each. Places change every shot.
FRIEND    0:35–1:05   6–8 shots. A second character built out of 3–4 wrong details.
RULE      1:05–1:20   3 shots. A town custom the hero explains wrongly.
BREAK     1:20–1:30   3 shots. He breaks the rule and finds out what it was really for.
THREAT    1:30–1:55   5 shots. Outsiders arrive. Fast cuts, 1.0–1.5 s.
BRAVE     1:55–2:10   4 shots. The hero does the one thing he was scared of.
PRICE     2:10–2:25   4 shots. The friend pays for it. An image, not a speech.
LIST      2:25–2:35   3 shots. The planted details, restated, then re-read.
REVERSE   2:35–2:45   3 shots. The threat turns out to be about the friend, not the town.
BIGGER    2:45–3:00   3 shots. A bigger threat, someone stays behind, the promise of part two.
```

Hard numbers that come out of that: **700–760 words, 175–185 seconds, 74–80 shots, 244–252 words a minute, median
shot 2.2–2.5 s, shortest shot under 1 s, longest under 5 s, 20–24 situations.**

## The 75% retention plan

Average view duration of 75% is a different target from "good film", so it needs its own rules:

1. **Test shorter cuts.** 75% of 2:55 is 2:11 of attention. 75% of 1:50 is 1:22. Cut the same story to 110 s and the
   same audience clears the bar. Build the next batch at 1:50–2:10, not 3:00.
2. **A loop point.** The last line should hand straight back to the first: "In this town, everybody's shadow copies
   them" after a final shot of a shadow copying him. A rewatch is a full view.
3. **Never let a shot be furniture.** If a shot only exists to show where we are, it is a cut point. Every shot
   carries a fact, a joke or a turn.
4. **The hook must be an impossible fact, not a feeling.** "I was scared of everything" is a feeling. "I was the only
   one who was not a monster" is a fact that cannot be true.
5. **One question on screen at all times.** Plant the next question in the shot before the current one is answered.
6. **Front-load the oddest image.** The first frame should be the thing a person would screenshot.
7. **Cut the abstractions.** No "it was a good life", no "nobody knew why". Concrete nouns only: bread, lantern,
   sword, scarf, basket, pudding, shoelace.

## The batch

Five films that reuse the machinery with new plots. All are one-shot, 110–130 s, 45–55 shots, 22–30 situations, and
each ends on a loop line rather than a cliffhanger (the owner's one-shot rule), except where marked.

1. **The Boy Who Was the Only One With a Door.** Every house in this town has no door — people walk through walls.
   His family has a door, and they lock it from the inside, and he has never been allowed to open it. Planted: the
   key he wears, the crack in the frame, the fact that nobody else can *see* the door. Reveal: the door is not his
   house's, it is his — it is the only way out of the town, and his family has been standing in front of it his
   whole life.
2. **My Shadow Is the Only Thing That Eats.** The town's rule is that shadows eat nothing. His eats everything, and
   the town starts leaving food out for it, then starts leaving *other* things. Reveal: the shadow is eating the town
   — the buildings are thinning, and the food was never for it.
3. **Everyone in This Town Has One More Birthday Than They Should.** He turns twelve and his family throws him a
   thirteenth. Concrete plant list: an extra candle, an extra chair, an extra plate, a name on the cake that is not
   his. Reveal: the extra birthday belongs to the brother who did not come back, and the town has been keeping him
   alive with cutlery.
4. **In This Town, the Rain Falls Up.** He is the only kid who gets wet. Rule + punchline engine for the first act,
   then: the rain is not rain, it is the town taking something back up, and the thing it takes is the thing he is
   carrying. Ends on the loop line.
5. **The Fastest Kid in This Town Has Never Touched the Ground.** (Series, two parts.) A kid who wins every race
   without a single footprint. Plants: clean shoes, no dirt, the way he stands. Reveal: he is a shadow that got out,
   and the finish line is where he has to choose a body.

Every one of these is written to the same skeleton: a rule the town obeys, a hero who is the exception, a custom he
misreads, three to five concrete plants, a threat from outside, one brave act, a list, a re-read, a loop.
