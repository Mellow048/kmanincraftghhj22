# Holding a viewer to the end of a "What if"

**Why this file exists.** In This Town's "Monster Village part one" held **81% of viewers to the end** — the channel's
best — and part four was rejected on story. Both were the same length, speed and cast. The difference was in what
each line *did*. That analysis is
[`../InThisTown/RETENTION.md`](../InThisTown/RETENTION.md), and it is worth reading in full: everything below that
is not about the power comes straight from it.

A "What if" film is a different animal from an In This Town story — it runs 60–118 s, it is about a power, and it
ends on an answer rather than a cliffhanger. But the thing that held 81% of an audience is not a genre trick. It is
this:

> **Every scene asks the viewer something. Nothing is merely reported.**

[`SCRIPT.md`](SCRIPT.md) is the beat sheet. This file is what to protect while you follow it.

---

## 1. The power is a question, not a feature

A "What if" gets its pull from one line: **what does this power cost?** The viewer works on that from the moment
the power appears.

- Say the **rule** in the first 15 seconds. "A heart over anybody who liked somebody. I could not see who it was for."
  The rule and its limit are the question. Without the limit the film is a list of wins, and a list of wins is
  something you can leave.
- The limit must be **shown early and paid late**. In the crush film: the limit is on screen in shot 4 and it is
  what the last line lands on. That is a promise kept at 90% of the runtime, and it is the reason to stay.
- **Never add a second power.** One look, one rule, one limit, for the whole film (`README.md`, principle 2). A
  second power resets the viewer's model, and a reset is where they leave.

## 2. The shape that held 81%, mapped onto the fourteen beats

`SCRIPT.md`'s beats already match part one's spine. These are the percentages to hit, and the job of each beat.

| Beat (`SCRIPT.md`) | % of film | The job | If it is missing, the viewer leaves at |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 The question (2 shots) | 0% | The power **in the picture** while the title is said. One odd thing, one person, one frame | second 1 |
| 2 The day it came (3) | 4% | Arrival, in one line. **One question opened** and left hanging | second 8 |
| 3 The rule (7) | 10% | Three fast demonstrations, then the limit shown once, lightly | second 20 |
| 4 The fun (6) | 22% | **Three wins, each funnier or bigger.** This is the goodwill the ending spends | second 30 |
| 5 The wish (3) | 32% | What he actually wants. A question, not a statement | ~40% |
| 6 The plant (4) | 37% | **The clue, said out loud and waved away.** The viewer keeps it | the reveal |
| 7 The first try (5) | 45% | Funny failure. The power is not a cheat code | ~50% |
| 8 The second try (5) | 55% | It works, **and something small is lost** — unnoticed by him | 60% |
| 9 The friend (3) | 62% | Somebody says the thing he will not hear | 65% |
| 10 The edge (6) | 70% | **The longest beat after the rule.** The worst moment, in full | the drop |
| 11 The act (3) | 82% | He chooses, and it costs him | 85% |
| 12 The wait (3) | 88% | Silence. No words. This is where part one's owners said "give it time" | 90% |
| 13 The reveal (6) | 92% | One short sentence, then why. **Answer the title with something it did not expect** | the end |
| 14 The button (3) | 99% | The subscribe line, over a picture | the last second |

**Two numbers from part one to steal:**

- **The first payoff lands at ~36% of the film**, not at the end. Part one's reversal ("they were standing in a ring,
  facing out") is a third of the way in. It tells the viewer this film answers things. Put one real answer before
  the halfway mark — in a "What if" that is usually the **limit doing something the viewer did not expect**.
- **The last quarter escalates twice**: the reveal, then the button. Do not let the film land flat after the reveal.

## 3. The five rules that keep people in

### R1. The teller is the subject, always

Part four lost its viewer because for 36 seconds the boy in a cage watched a fight, and the narration said "I did not
see that either." The same failure in a "What if" is the **power performing while the kid watches**: a montage of
hearts appearing over heads, with nobody choosing anything.

- Every use of the power is a **choice somebody makes**, on screen, with a cost.
- No "and then I saw hundreds of hearts." He uses it, or he refuses to.

### R2. A question is open by second 20, and named by 30%

Part one opens one at 0:24 and *names* it at 0:47. Part four opens none, and stops at 1:05.

In a "What if", the question is nearly always **the limit**. Say it early, out loud, and let the viewer spend the
middle of the film testing it. "I could not see who the heart was for" is a question the viewer can play with for
90 seconds.

### R3. Jokes are the pace, not the decoration

Part one's first 25 seconds are five jokes. They are not there to be funny; they buy the goodwill the mystery
spends. A "What if" with a rule and no jokes feels like a maths lesson.

The crush film's version: the heart that is bigger than the person, the teacher's heart, the dog. **Three laughs
before the wish.**

### R4. Somebody speaks

Part one had 10 lines of dialogue; part four had 6 and four were the teller alone. A voice that is not the teller's
makes the film feel populated. In a "What if", the friend and the crush should each get one line the viewer
remembers.

### R5. Cut on the word, and never leave the close-up for long

`OWNER.md` Rule B applies unchanged: no two neighbouring shots alike; every scene has an environment shot, a
travelling shot and an odd angle; the cut lands on the word. The crush film's median shot is 1.97 s against part
one's 2.37 s: a "What if" can afford to be faster, not looser.

## 4. The 1080×1920 specific rules

- **The power must be readable at phone size in one glance.** A heart the size of a grape cannot carry a shot. This
  is why `HEART` starts at 0.13 m and why the rule sizes it by distance — the size *is* the storytelling.
- **The look of the power never changes** (`README.md`). Recognition is retention: by 30% the viewer must be able to
  say "that is a heart" in one frame.
- **The teller wears one loud colour** so a phone can find him in a full classroom. Every second a viewer spends
  looking for him is a second they consider leaving.

## 5. Before you write

- [ ] What does the viewer **want to know** by second 15?
- [ ] What is the **rule**, and what is the **one thing it cannot do**?
- [ ] What are the **three jokes** before the wish?
- [ ] What is **planted** at beat 6, and which line pays it?
- [ ] What is the **first real answer**, and is it before 40%?
- [ ] What does **the title mean** at the end that it did not mean at the start?

## 6. Before you build

- [ ] The teller makes every use of the power a **choice**, and there is no montage of wins he did not choose.
- [ ] The limit is said **out loud** by 15 seconds, and paid in the last 10.
- [ ] Three jokes before the wish. Count them.
- [ ] Somebody who is not the teller speaks at least twice.
- [ ] No two neighbouring shots share framing *and* move.
- [ ] The reveal gets **silence**: no words over it (`OWNER.md`, the helmet film lesson).
- [ ] The last line contains "subscribe" and the picture is not a title card.

## 7. After you build: the two numbers

1. **Stayed to watch.** Under 70%: find the timestamp where it fell, and look at that stretch against §2. It is
   almost always a beat that reported instead of asked.
2. **The 1:05 test.** Watch 1:00–1:20. If the teller is watching somebody else use the power, it will fail there.
