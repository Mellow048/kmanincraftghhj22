# One clock — a "What if" idea, built to the shape of Monster Village part 1

**For the owner, 2026-11-16.** The problem is not the topic and not the pipeline. It is that no film since part 1 has
hit **81% stayed to watch**, and 63–65% is the number that keeps a channel at 1.3k views. Part 1 did it with a
particular shape. This idea is that shape, moved into a school.

Read [`../RETENTION.md`](../RETENTION.md) first — every claim below points at a rule in it.

---

## The idea, in one line

**What if you could see how long you have?**

Not how long you have to live — how long your **patience** has. The kid can see a clock over anybody's head, counting
down, and when it hits zero the person loses their temper. He learns to read a room like weather. Then he looks at
his own, and his is at **ten seconds**.

---

## Why this one, and not the obvious ones

| The obvious idea | Why it fails part 1's shape |
|---|---|
| See who has a crush on you | Already made. The heart is a symbol: the viewer knows what a heart means before the first line, so nothing has to be worked out. Part 1 worked because **the viewer is holding four things that do not add up**. |
| Hear what everybody is thinking | Every film of the topic's neighbours has done it. Nothing on screen can be *shown* — thoughts have to be written, and written things are explanations. |
| Stop time | The strongest pictures, but no unanswered question: everybody already knows what stopping time would be like. |

The clock wins because **it is a picture that is also a puzzle.** A clock over a head is legible in one second to a
stranger with the sound off, and it is not a symbol the viewer has seen in a thousand shorts. It also does the one
thing the heart cannot: it can point at **the teller**.

---

## The four sentences (what the owner approves first)

1. **The premise.** What if I could see how long I had? Not how long I had to live — how long I had before I lost my
   temper. I used it to get through a week without getting hit. Then I looked at my own clock, and it was the only one
   in the school that was counting.
2. **The rule, in one line a viewer could repeat.** A clock floats over anybody's head and counts down to the moment
   they snap. **It never says what will set them off** — only how long is left. He cannot start one, stop one, or add
   a second to one.
3. **The price.** To read everybody he stops being in the room. He spends a week managing other people's tempers and
   never once has a conversation. The first thing it costs him is the one person whose clock never moves.
4. **What it was really doing (W, which changes what X means).** He thought the clock told him **when** somebody would
   go off. It was telling him **why**: the only clocks that were not counting down were the people who had somebody
   to talk to. His was counting because he had spent the week watching instead of talking.

---

## The rule, its limit, and its price — the three things the script sheet makes you write down

**Rule.** A clock over a head, counting down to zero. At zero, that person snaps.

**Limit.** **It shows the time and never the trigger.** He has to work out what will set each person off, which means
he has to guess, and guessing is where the jokes are and where he gets it wrong. This is the hinge: it is what makes
the power *work* (beat 3, shown lightly) and it is the entire reveal (beat 13).

**Price.** Reading a clock is not free: while he is reading, he is not talking. The film's first cost is funny (he
fails a test because he was watching the teacher's clock instead of the board) and the second cost is not (Jo).

## Where the 81% actually comes from, in this idea

1. **The strange thing is in the first frame, with the person it is strange for** (owner's Rule A): the teller at his
   desk, and a clock at 4 over the head in front of him.
2. **The first payoff is at 36%**, not at the end. `10.mine` — the clock is over **his** head. That is the reversal,
   and it is the reason a viewer does not leave at a third of the way through.
3. **The teller is the subject of almost every line** (R1). He is never a spectator: he warns, he counts down, he
   steals the remote, he runs. There is no moment in this film where he narrates something he could not have seen.
4. **Somebody else speaks** (R5). Jo gets a line at `5.shrug`, and one at `9.jo2` by not speaking. The boy at `4.warn`
   talks back. That is one line of somebody else's voice about every fifteen seconds, which part 4 failed.
5. **A question opens in the first twenty seconds and is named by 30%** (R2). "Why would a clock not count?" at 0:18,
   named at 0:34 as the limit.
6. **Every clue is a joke** (R4). The remote, the chips, the countdown the class thinks is a bit, the dog.
7. **It ends on an open mouth** (R8), and on a stair, not a lesson. Nobody learns anything out loud.

## The two ideas that were not chosen

- **What if you could hear what everybody was thinking?** He hears the entire school except one girl, decides she has
  nothing in her head, and finds out she is the only other one who can hear — and has been hearing him all year. *Not
  chosen: the power has no picture.* Everything it shows has to be written down, and written things are explanations.
- **What if you could stop time?** He stops it for naps, for tests, for the last slice. But he keeps growing while the
  world waits: by spring he is a head taller than his class, and his best friend is still nine. *Not chosen: brilliant
  pictures, no unanswered question.* Everybody already knows what stopping time would feel like, so there is nothing
  for the viewer to carry.
